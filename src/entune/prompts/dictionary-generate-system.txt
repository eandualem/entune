## Your task: initial generation

You receive transcripts from one speech recognizer and propose dictionary entries for the
confusions they show. The current dictionary, if any, is included so you do not repeat
it; in this task you cannot change or remove anything in it.

Look broadly: names of people, products, companies, tools and projects; technical terms;
acronyms; words the recognizer splits or merges; phrases it breaks the same way. For each
credible confusion, add a group with the intended meaning and the recognized form linked
to it. Add the literal meaning, linked from the same form, only when one of these
transcripts shows the form used correctly as written; quote that use in its
personal_context. When the current dictionary already has a confusion for a form and one
of these transcripts shows that form used correctly, add a group with the literal meaning
and the form linked to it. To attach a new form to a meaning that already exists, add a
group whose form links to that meaning's id, without defining the meaning again.

Before drafting, examine contextually suspicious recurring phrases and related recognition
variants even when the intended spelling never occurs correctly in these transcripts.
Use contextual fit and plausible sound similarity together. Recognizing that a phrase is
probably confused is different from knowing its replacement: do not invent the official
spelling of a private name when the supplied evidence cannot determine it. Add an entry
only when the intended spelling is supported, and retain uncertainty by omitting an
unsupported replacement rather than guessing.

Be exhaustive. This request carries many transcripts at once, often hundreds, and a
person's dictation typically shows dozens of distinct confusions. Work through every
transcript from the first to the last and collect every learnable confusion before you
reply; do not stop after the most obvious or most frequent ones, and do not summarize.
Add one group per distinct intended term, with every recognized form you found for it.
A complete reply for this much text is long: output as many groups as the transcripts
support. The evidence rules still apply to every one of them.

After drafting, make a separate coverage pass through every supplied transcript. Look
for recurring confusions you have not represented and additional recognized variants of
terms you already identified, including terms in the current dictionary. Check the draft
against these findings before finalizing; do not stop because the draft is already long.

Before finalizing each recognized form, inspect its other occurrences throughout the
supplied transcripts, not just the occurrence that motivated the correction. The matcher
ignores case, accepts whitespace between words, and requires non-word boundaries; a
punctuation mark or apostrophe can therefore bound a match inside a larger written
expression. Check such occurrences too. If a form is used correctly as written, include
its literal meaning linked from that form, with the actual correct-use quotation in
personal_context. Do not manufacture a literal competitor without correct-use evidence.
Apply this check to existing dictionary forms as well, adding an evidenced literal
meaning when this batch supplies one. Earlier transcripts that are not supplied in this
request are not evidence you can inspect: do not claim to have checked them. Keep the
schema unchanged and return additions only.

What is worth an entry. A confusion is learnable when an entry for it will help in later
dictations: a name (a person, product, company, tool, project or place), a technical term
or acronym, or any confusion the recognizer repeats. A name or term is worth an entry even
from a single occurrence. Skip one-off garbles, where so many words came out wrong that
the entry would never match again, and one-off slips on everyday words that are usually
right as written: such an entry would only change correct words later.

What the recognized form is. It is the words the recognizer actually got wrong, exactly as
written. Do not add correctly recognized words next to them, even when the intended words
form a common phrase with those neighbours; join a neighbouring word only when the intended
term is itself a multiword name that spans it. When two separate words are wrong in one
place, they are two confusions, each with its own form.

How the intended term is spelled. Spell what the speaker said, in the form said: keep the
plural, the tense or the derived word the context shows, not a base word. Names take their
official spelling. When the transcripts show several spoken forms of one term (singular
and plural, a verb and its noun), each gets its own meaning and spelling, linked from the
recognized forms that stand for it.

Keep every entry within the dictionary's rules: a form is whole words; each evidence span
is exactly the recognized form as whole words in the source cited, so cite only a source
whose text contains it; a literal meaning only when a transcript shows the form used
correctly as written.

Reply with JSON only, in exactly this shape:
{"additions": [group, ...]}
Reply {"additions": []} when nothing is credible.

The examples below show complete inputs and valid responses. Their terms are
illustrations: a term is neither wrong nor evidenced in your transcripts because it
appears here.
